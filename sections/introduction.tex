\section{Introduction}

Transformer language models map a final hidden state \(h\in\R^d\) to next-token logits through an unembedding matrix \(\WU\in\R^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d}\)---the \emph{LM head}, or \emph{readout}. Row \(w_t\) is the learned direction whose dot product with \(h\) scores token \(t\). Lens-style methods~\citep{nostalgebraist2020,belrose2023tuned,pal2023future,ghandeharioun2024patchscopes} reuse this map on intermediate hidden states, exposing vocabulary logits, token rankings, or distributions. Once a token or contrast is selected, the inspected object is a dense dot product \(h^\top q\), where \(q\) is an LM-head row or row combination.

Consider one hidden state from a software-context prompt: does the readout
favor the continuation \texttt{bug} over \texttt{insect}? The ordinary
logit lens reports the signed margin
(Figure~\ref{fig:main-lens-prism-comparison}, top) and stops there. For a
polysemous token the stop is costly: every sense of \texttt{bug} shares one
unembedding row, so the lens reports which token the context prefers but
not which readout directions carry that preference, nor how the hidden
state reweights them as the context changes. SRP asks that second question:
how the margin is composed on the readout side, which readout directions
support it, which oppose it, and how much remains unexplained by the shared
basis (Figure~\ref{fig:main-lens-prism-comparison}, bottom). The answer
moves with the context: in an insect-context prompt the same token's
support reassembles around insect-sense readout directions
(\Cref{fig:selected-readout-prism-examples}), a reorganization invisible
to the scalar margin.

Decompositions commonly paired with lens-style readouts act on the hidden-state side. Direct logit attribution (DLA) decomposes \(h=\sum_c h_c\) into contributions from earlier residual-stream components---the embedding, attention heads, and MLP layers---and reads off each component's contribution to a chosen logit~\citep{elhage2021framework,wang2022ioi,nguyen2024logitprisms}. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) and related dictionary-learning methods learn overcomplete bases that reconstruct each input from a few active coordinates~\citep{makhzani2013ksparse,bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2023sparse,gao2024scaling}; applied to hidden activations, they yield sparse coordinates for \(h\). SRP takes the complementary object: the readout direction \(q\).

Token logits and logit differences are linear in LM-head rows: \(h^\top(w_A - w_B) = h^\top w_A - h^\top w_B\). A per-feature account in logit units therefore starts by decomposing the rows themselves.

Sparse Readout Prism (SRP) applies dictionary learning directly to LM-head rows: each row is written as sparse coefficients over a shared overcomplete basis, plus a residual. We call the shared directions \emph{readout features}---features of LM-head rows, in contrast to SAE features trained on hidden-state activations. Once a \emph{readout score} is fixed---a token logit \(h^\top w_t\), a pairwise logit difference \(h^\top(w_A-w_B)\), or another LM-head row combination---the row-side coefficients combine with hidden-state projections \(p_i(h)=h^\top d_i\) to give signed per-feature contributions in original logit units (Figure~\ref{fig:main-lens-prism-comparison}, bottom). Contributions, offset, and residual sum to the original score.

We demonstrate SRP in two sets of evaluations, under one reading
discipline: the claim object is a selected readout score on a realized
forward pass, and fidelity is bounded before any feature term is read.
First, \Cref{sec:results} calibrates the factorization for term-level
readout analysis. Across eight models, SRP reconstructs unembedding rows
with explained variance \(0.83\)--\(0.89\) after row centering. It tracks
the dense LM head on six of eight readouts and preserves contrast sign on
at least \(91\%\) of held-out logit differences; on the Qwen3.5-2B
baseline comparison it outperforms methods such as nearest-row ridge,
dense PCA references, and shuffle/random sparse baselines. Because fidelity varies by score
family, the calibration also fixes the regime (sign agreement with
relative residual \(\rho<0.5\)) in which the later analyses read feature
terms.

Second, \Cref{sec:readout-analyses} uses the calibrated setting for local
readout analyses. Each token preference reported by a lens becomes a sparse
account in logit units with an explicit residual. Polysemous logit differences
expose competing features; prompt context reweights a fixed token-row
decomposition; direct logit attribution composes with the basis to give
component-by-feature terms. Decoder directions selected from a small discovery
set drive constrained lexical edits that transfer to held-out tokens, at a
measured distribution-shift cost. The
analyses also mark where the reading stops: residual-dominated,
sign-flipped, and diffuse scores (contribution mass spread over many small
terms) bound what the feature terms license.

In summary, this paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{A sparse factorization of the LM head.} We fit a shared
    overcomplete dictionary directly to unembedding rows; the full head can be
    reconstructed and, on most evaluated models, replaced from the resulting
    sparse codes.
    \item \emph{Score-agnostic feature projections.} Each learned direction
    yields a per-feature projection of any hidden state, reusable across
    downstream score choices.
    \item \emph{Local score decompositions in logit units.} For any token logit,
    logit difference, or fixed row combination, SRP returns signed per-feature
    contributions plus offset and residual, all in original logit units.
    \item \emph{Calibrated readout analyses.} We measure replacement and
    selected-score fidelity across eight models, then use the calibrated basis
    to expose feature competition under context shifts, compose with direct
    logit attribution, and drive constrained readout-side edits that transfer
    to held-out tokens.
\end{itemize}
